The part software can fix, and the part it cannot

Shake shows up in two places at once. The camera moves between frames, which puts the subject in a different spot each time — that is the jitter you see on playback, and software corrects it well by shifting and rotating frames to follow a smoothed path.

The camera also moves during each exposure, which smears that individual frame. No amount of realignment undoes it. Stabilise footage shot at a slow shutter speed and you get a rock-steady shot made of soft frames, which some people find more unsettling than the original shake, because the eye expects blur to accompany movement. If your clip looks smeared when paused, that is the ceiling on what stabilising can achieve.

Stabilising always costs framing

To cancel movement the tool has to push each frame around, and that leaves empty wedges at the edges. Every stabiliser hides them the same way: by cropping inward and scaling back up. The smoother you ask the result to be, the more it has to crop.

Two practical consequences. First, anything close to the edge of frame can be cropped out entirely, so check your composition afterwards rather than assuming it survived. Second, the crop costs resolution — a 4K clip stabilised hard can land near 1080p in real detail. If you know in advance that a shot will need it, frame looser than feels right and give the tool room to work.

Jello is a different problem

Most phone and mirrorless cameras use a rolling shutter: the sensor is read one line at a time, top to bottom, so the bottom of the frame is captured a fraction later than the top. Pan quickly and vertical lines lean; lampposts and door frames appear to bend and sway. This is a distortion inside the frame rather than a misplacement of it, so ordinary stabilisation only softens it. The reliable fixes are at capture time — pan more slowly, use a faster shutter, or use a camera with a mechanical or global shutter. The free option worth trying first

ffmpeg's vidstab pair analyses the clip in one pass and applies the correction in a second. It is free, it is genuinely good, and on moderate handheld shake it competes with paid tools. The trade-off is that you drive it from the command line and tune the smoothing by editing numbers and re-rendering, which gets tedious when you are hunting for the setting that fixes the wobble without gutting the framing. If the shot matters and you want to see the result as you adjust it, that is where a graphical tool earns its keep.

How to judge the result

  • Watch the edges, not the centre. Errors show up first as drift or a slow swim near the border of frame.
  • Check straight lines. Door frames, poles and horizons reveal warping that a moving subject will hide.
  • Look for over-correction. A shot locked perfectly still can look artificial when the framing implies handheld. Some residual movement usually reads better than none.
  • Compare at full size. The crop is invisible on a phone-sized preview and obvious on a television.
