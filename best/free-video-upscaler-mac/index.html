What the free command-line route costs, measured

The free frame route needs still images first. We measured what that means for disk space by upscaling real 1080p frames 2× with a Real-ESRGAN-family model. A 1080p frame saved as PNG was 1.52 MB on average. The same frame upscaled to 4K was 8.42 MB.

One minute of 1080p at 30 fps, upscaled to 4KScratch space needed
Source frames as PNG, 1,800 frames2.7 GB
Upscaled frames as PNG15.2 GB
Total before you re-encode17.9 GB

Then comes the time: a fast 2× model took 0.72 to 0.82 seconds a 4K frame on a Mac Studio, about 23 minutes for that one minute, and heavier models take longer. After that you still re-encode the frames into a video and put the audio back, and each of those is a command you have to get right.

Crisp does the same work in 20-second segments and deletes each one's frames before starting the next. Worked out from the same frame sizes, the frames it holds on disk peak at about 7 GB for a 1080p-to-4K export, however long the clip is; only the finished video grows with its length. That figure is calculated from how the app is built, not measured on a long render.

Try a short sample of an old home video

I make Crisp. For a family recording, the useful question is whether the people still look like themselves while they move. A sharper paused frame alone does not answer that.

  1. Keep the capture. Start with a digital file from the tape or camcorder. Keep that source unchanged and work on a copy. Trim to a moment up to 15 seconds long that has a moving face, a camera pan and some fine texture, if your clip has them. A still title card will not show what happens to motion.
  2. Check the shape and moving edges. If people look stretched, fix the picture's shape before judging sharpness. Enlarging a wide face only makes a bigger wide face. Comb teeth along a moving arm need care too: sharpening those stripes can make them harder to remove. Crisp checks for combing before an ordinary Enhance or upscale. Check the result in motion before doing more work.
  3. Try Enhance first. Drop the trimmed copy into Crisp, use Enhance, leave the choice on Auto and press Run. Use the Mac upscaling guide for the controls and scale choices. Starting with a short cut spends the free export on the part you want to judge.
  4. Watch the same moment in Before / After. Check faces at full size, then play the sample. Look for waxy skin, fabric that changes pattern and a wall or sky that shimmers. Check that the moving edge no longer has comb teeth. If the result smears movement or changes a face, keep the source unchanged and use it instead. Try a plain resize only if you need larger dimensions. More pixels are not worth losing the face you remember.

Questions about this comparison

Is there a free AI video upscaler for Mac?

Yes, of two kinds. Real-ESRGAN is free and open source; it works on still frames, so a video is split into frames and joined back together. Crisp's free version upscales with AI on your Mac, offline and with no account; its free AI exports stop at 15 seconds and carry a small crispvideo.app mark. The table above lists every route with its limits.

Can I choose which 15 seconds to upscale?

Yes. Trim the clip to the part you want first. The limit is per export, so choose a moment with movement and texture before running Enhance.

Can Crisp upscale a whole tape for free?

No. AI exports stop at 15 seconds. Pro adds full-length AI exports and removes the mark. Convert and Compress still run at full length for free, with the small "crispvideo.app" mark on video and GIF exports.

Does a free editor download include its AI enlargement tools?

A download alone does not establish the AI allowance. Read the feature and limit columns, then confirm the current plan before starting a long tape.

Why does a frame-based video route need so much disk space?

It holds many separate images before the output is encoded. Our disk figures describe that workflow for the frame sizes above. A different format or clip can use a different amount of space.
