Normalize vs the Volume setting

The Volume setting multiplies the loudness by a factor you choose (2× louder, 0.5× quieter). Normalize is automatic. It measures the loudness and matches it to a consistent -16 LUFS (EBU R128) target, the common streaming/social level with headroom so it doesn't clip. Reach for Normalize when the levels are inconsistent and you don't want to guess a number; reach for Volume when you simply want it louder or quieter.

About matching loudness

Peak normalisation and loudness are not the same thing

Peak normalisation just scales the file until its single loudest sample hits the ceiling. One stray clap can therefore leave a quiet interview barely louder than it started. Loudness normalisation measures perceived volume across the whole clip instead, which is what actually makes two videos sound level next to each other.

Platforms normalise on their own terms anyway: YouTube targets around -14 LUFS, and anything louder gets turned down on playback. Mastering hotter than that buys you nothing except less dynamic range.

“Make the quiet parts louder”: what it does, measured

We measured it so you can disagree with the numbers. A 12-second test file (pink noise band-limited to 180 to 3,800 Hz, roughly the speech band, in alternating 1.5-second quiet and loud blocks) went through Crisp’s audio lane in level mode and was measured block by block with ffmpeg’s volumedetect. A synthetic signal on purpose, to isolate the level behaviour; the figures are means across the four quiet and four loud blocks. The quiet blocks went from -43.08 dB to -27.10 dB (+15.98 dB). The loud blocks went from -21.15 dB to -10.30 dB (+10.85 dB). The gap between them closed from 21.93 dB to 16.80 dB, only 5.13 dB. So the quiet talking comes up by about 16 dB, which is why anyone presses the button, but the loud parts rise 10.85 dB too.

The whole operation is one short ffmpeg chain: acompressor=threshold=-22dB:ratio=4:attack=20:release=300:makeup=6,alimiter=limit=0.95. The makeup of 6 is a linear multiplier (+15.56 dB) applied to every sample, and that is the entire lift: with the ratio set to 1 so nothing is compressed, -43.0 dB still became -27.4 dB. The compressor only touches audio above -22 dBFS, so the quiet blocks take the full makeup gain and nothing else, while the loud blocks take 4:1 gain reduction that claws back about 5 dB, which is exactly the gap that closed.

What it costs. The noise floor comes up with everything else: room hum, air conditioning and tape hiss live in the quiet passages, so they rise the same 16 dB the dialogue does. Clean first, level second. The output lands just under the ceiling (the loudest sample reads about -0.4 dBFS), so little headroom is left for anything stacked afterwards, and there is no strength slider: the filter values are fixed. Heavy compression flattens a performance. What it will not do. It cannot lift the voice and leave the music: Crisp has no stem separation, so every audio mode acts on the mixed soundtrack, and it says so in its own words. It works a whole clip at a time, so to level one stretch, split the clip where the change should start (press S, or right-click, Split here) and apply it to that piece.

The noun picks the mode. The plain-English box maps a request onto one of fifteen audio modes. “Level the audio”, “level the volume” and “even out the sound” run Normalize. “Level the dialogue”, “level the voices”, “level the dynamics”, “make the quiet parts louder” and “the quiet parts are too quiet” run Level audio. The verb does not decide; the noun does.
