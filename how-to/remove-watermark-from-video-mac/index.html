How to remove a watermark from a video on a Mac

Crisp · built in Grand Rapids · published 2026-08-14 · last updated 2026-11-11
Quick answer

To remove a watermark from a video on a Mac, the honest answer first: nothing removes a burned-in watermark cleanly. Once a logo has been written into the picture it is the same kind of pixel as the face next to it, and every method below is a way of hiding it rather than lifting it off. Which one you want depends on what sits behind the mark.

Only remove a watermark from video you own or have the rights to change: your own logo, your own exports, or footage whose licence allows it. Removing a creator’s or stock library’s mark from their work can break copyright law.

Why it cannot simply be switched off

When an editor puts a logo on a clip, the logo lives on its own layer and the export flattens everything into one image. That flattening is the point at which the information is lost. The finished file does not record that a logo was ever separate, any more than a photograph records which object the photographer was standing behind.

So there is no undo. What is left is the question of what to put in the affected region, and there are only three answers anyone has ever found.

The three methods, and what each one costs

MethodWorks whenWhat you give up
Crop it outThe mark sits in a corner or along an edgeFraming and resolution. A corner crop on 1080p can leave you near 720p.
Cover itYou control where the video will be shownNothing technically, but you have swapped one visible block for another.
Inpaint itThe background behind it is plain and mostly stillSharpness in that patch, and stability if anything moves through it.

Cropping is the only one of the three that is not a guess, which is why it is worth considering first even though it sounds crude. If the logo occupies the bottom right eighth of the frame and your subject is centred, a crop costs you resolution you can partly buy back on export. That is a known, bounded loss. The other two trade a certainty for a maybe.

What inpainting is actually doing

An inpainting tool masks the region you mark and fills it using two sources: the pixels immediately around the hole, and the same region in nearby frames where the background may have been briefly visible. On a static shot over a plain wall this is close to invisible, because both sources agree with each other and with the truth.

Put the same tool over grass, water, a moving crowd or a camera pan and the two sources stop agreeing. The fill is then recomputed frame by frame from changing evidence, so the patch shimmers even when each individual frame looks passable paused. That shimmer is the thing viewers notice, and it is why results that look fine in a screenshot disappoint in motion.

The practical test takes ten seconds. Scrub to the busiest moment in the clip, look at the region behind the mark, and ask whether you could describe what should be there. If you cannot, no software can either.

Do the repair before any upscale, never after

This order matters more than the choice of method. An upscaler is trained to reconstruct edges it believes are real, and it has no way of knowing you consider one particular set of edges to be the enemy. Feed it a watermarked frame and it will hand back a crisper watermark.

Repair first, then upscale, and accept that the repaired region will be the softest part of the finished frame, because it is the only part built from a guess rather than from the sensor.

Where Crisp fits

Crisp does not remove watermarks, and it says so when you ask rather than running something and hoping. It works the other direction: it can add a watermark, caption or border to footage on export, so if you are the one protecting a clip this is the side of the job it handles.

Once a mark has been dealt with elsewhere, bring the file here for the parts that are not a guess. Denoise, stabilise, colour, and an upscale that puts back the resolution a crop cost you. Those all work from real detail that is present in the frame.

One more thing worth checking before any of this: if the clip is yours, look for the project file or the original camera file first. A re-export takes a minute and beats every method on this page, and people reach for a repair tool surprisingly often when the clean master is still sitting in a folder.
