Great for constant noise — gentle on voices

Denoise targets the steady stuff: tape hiss, an electrical hum, an AC unit or fan, low-end rumble. It's tuned conservatively so speech and music stay natural rather than getting that underwater, over-processed sound. It won't erase a one-off door slam or a passing car, but a constant noise floor drops noticeably. Want the levels even too? Run Normalize after.

Audio noise vs video noise

Two different "noise" problems, two different tools:

  • Background noise in the sound — hiss, hum, AC drone. That's this guide: the Audio lane's Denoise mode.
  • Visual noise / grain in the picture — speckle in dark or low-light footage. For that, use Enhance / Low-light instead.

Steady noise comes out cleanly; sudden noise does not

Noise reduction works by learning a constant noise profile — air conditioning, a computer fan, traffic hum — and subtracting it. That's why steady background noise disappears almost completely while a door slam, a cough or a chair scrape survives untouched. Those are edits, not filter problems.

Push denoising too hard and voices develop a watery, underwater artefact as the filter removes parts of the speech along with the noise. If the room tone vanishes entirely and the gaps between words are pure digital silence, you've gone too far: a little residual hiss sounds more natural than none at all.

Fix it at the source when you can

Every processing option is damage control. Moving a mic a foot closer to the speaker improves the ratio of voice to room noise more than any filter, because you gain signal instead of removing noise. Turning off an air conditioner for a take costs nothing and removes the problem entirely.

Room tone is a tool, not a defect

Record thirty seconds of the room with nobody speaking. That clean sample of the exact noise you're fighting gives a denoiser a profile to work from, and it also lets you patch gaps in an edit so silences match the rest of the scene rather than dropping to nothing.
