Wind noise on a Mac: the rumble drops 13 dB, the voice stays

Quick answer

Wind on a mic is mostly low-frequency rumble, and a high-pass filter removes most of it without touching a voice. We measured Crisp's one-tap Wind mode on a voice mixed with gusty wind: the rumble below 150 Hz, 85% of the wind's energy, fell 12.9 dB, while the voice moved 0.2 dB. The breathy "whoosh" above 150 Hz overlaps the voice and only fell 2 to 3.5 dB, so no filter of this kind removes it. It all runs on your Mac and the picture is copied untouched.

What Wind mode removes, measured

We built a test we could repeat. A spoken sentence (macOS's own speech voice) was mixed with synthetic wind at equal loudness. The wind was mostly deep rumble plus a quieter, gusty "whoosh" between 200 and 1,000 Hz, because real wind is not only rumble. Before the filter, 84.8% of the wind's energy sat below 150 Hz, 7.6% between 150 and 500 Hz, and 7.6% above 500 Hz. We ran the voice alone, the wind alone and the mix through Wind mode on the installed app and measured each band.

Read across the rows. The deep rumble loses about 95% of its energy (12.9 dB), and it was 85% of the wind. The voice keeps its level where speech lives: 0.2 dB is below what anyone can hear. What the filter can't reach is the whoosh above 150 Hz, which only fell 2 to 3.5 dB, because it shares frequencies with the voice. Overall the wind came down 9.1 dB, to about an eighth of its energy.

The limits, plainly: this is one voice and synthetic wind, not every microphone and every gust. A deep voice has more of its sound near 150 Hz than this one does, so it may lose a little more warmth. And wind that overloads the microphone, the crackly bursts described below, is past what any filter can fix.

Wind vs Denoise: which mode?

Both live in the Audio lane, but they solve different problems:

  • Wind — the loud, low rumble of wind buffeting a mic outdoors. Firm high-pass. That's this guide.
  • Denoise — steady broadband hiss, hum, or AC drone in a room. Gentler, broadband. See remove background noise.
  • Visual noise / grain in the picture — that's not audio at all. Use Enhance / Low-light.

Wind noise questions

How do I remove wind noise from a video on a Mac for free?

Use Crisp: drop your clip in, select the clip and use Audio in the Tools panel, then choose Wind (or type "reduce the wind noise"), and press it. It cuts the wind on your Mac: no account, no upload.

How does it cut wind noise?

Wind on a mic is mostly low-frequency rumble, so Wind mode starts with a high-pass around 150 Hz, under most speech, then a light denoiser for what is left. Measured: the rumble below 150 Hz fell 12.9 dB while the voice moved 0.2 dB in the speech band.

Can it remove all the wind?

No. It removes most of the deep rumble, which is most of the wind: 12.9 dB in our test. The breathy whoosh that overlaps a voice only drops 2 to 3.5 dB, because filtering it out would take the voice with it. A windscreen when you record is the only fix for that part.

Is this different from Denoise?

Yes — Denoise is for steady broadband hiss/hum/AC drone; Wind is tuned for the loud low rumble of wind on a mic. Pick Wind for breezy outdoor footage.

Does it change the video?

No — only the audio is processed; the picture is stream-copied, so there's no quality loss and it's fast.

Wind is a low-frequency problem

Wind noise lives almost entirely at the bottom of the spectrum: the rumble and buffeting from air hitting the mic capsule directly. That's why a high-pass filter rolling off below roughly 80–120Hz removes a surprising amount of it while leaving speech intact, since almost no useful information in a human voice sits down there.

What can't be fixed is a fully overloaded capsule. When wind physically clips the mic, the recording has no clean signal underneath to recover. That's why a foam windscreen outdoors saves footage that no amount of processing will.

Physical protection beats every filter

A foam cover handles light breeze; a furry windjammer handles real wind, and the difference between them outdoors is not subtle. Even cupping a hand upwind of a phone mic measurably helps. None of this is a processing tip, but it's the difference between footage you can rescue and footage you can't.

Check whether there's anything left to save

Before spending time on a clip, listen to a section where nobody is speaking. If you can hear the room and the wind sitting on top of it, there's clean signal underneath and filtering will help. If the wind gusts produce a hard, crackly distortion that cuts out entirely between buffets, the capsule was overloaded and the underlying audio wasn't recorded at all.

In that case the honest options are re-recording the audio, replacing it with narration, or cutting to a different angle, not a stronger filter.
