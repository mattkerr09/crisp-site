Two kinds of pixelated, and why the difference matters

People say "pixelated" for two faults that look alike and are fixed differently. The first is low resolution, stretched: a 480p clip or an old phone video enlarged to fill a modern screen. Every frame is soft and square-edged, still frames as much as moving ones, because there were simply not many pixels to begin with. The second is compression blocking, sometimes called macroblocking: the encoder ran short of bits and spent them in squares, so blocks appear in movement, in dark areas and across smooth gradients like skies, and some frames are worse than others.

The quick test is to pause on a moment where nothing moves. If the paused frame looks clean and the blocks only appear when the picture changes, you are looking at compression. If even a still frame shows visible squares, it is resolution. Old phone footage usually has both at once, which is why it looks as bad as it does.

Step 1: find the best copy before fixing anything

Every re-save costs detail, and nothing downstream can recover what an earlier copy threw away. A clip that was sent through a messaging app, downloaded from a social network or screen-recorded has usually been re-compressed at least once, often at a lower resolution than it was filmed. If the original still exists ( in iCloud Photos, on the camera's card, or with the person who filmed it) start from that. Ten minutes of looking for the original is worth more than any setting further down this page.

Step 2: rebuild the detail on your Mac

  1. Drop in the video. Drag the clip onto Crisp's window. It reads the file and checks its resolution automatically.
  2. Let AUTO choose, or pick Enhance. AUTO picks the model and the scale. To choose yourself, pick Enhance and a scale of 2×, 3×, 4×, 8× or 16×. The big steps exist for very small sources such as early phone video, and Crisp greys out the scales your clip cannot be encoded at.
  3. Export. Crisp upscales, cleans and sharpens on-device and saves a new file beside your original. Nothing is uploaded, and the original is left as it was.

For the clips that matter most (the only video of a wedding, a grandparent, a first birthday) the generative Max lane in Crisp Pro rebuilds detail far more aggressively. It is slow, and on video it needs a Mac with 48 GB of memory or more (a single photo needs 16 GB).

What the rebuild actually recovers, measured

To put a number on it we made a pixelated clip on purpose: a sharp public-domain clip reduced to what a mid-2000s camera phone recorded (176×144 pixels, 15 fps, H.263 at 64 kbps) and then restored all 38 frames of it. Against a plain upscale to the same size, Crisp's Max lane held 1.93× the fine detail. It is on the home page as a before/after you can drag. The damage was chosen, and real damage is rarely so tidy, which is why the number is a ceiling rather than a promise.

A second measurement, on real frames halved and then upscaled back to full size: a plain resize restored at most 64% of the fine detail, and a heavier AI model up to 96%, by drawing it, which is the honest way to say what an upscaler does (how that was measured). And the cost in time, on a 3.75-second film clip of 90 frames: the free Enhance lane upscaled it 4× in 13.0 seconds, while Max took 30.3 seconds a frame, 209 times slower. That gap is why Enhance is the default.

What no tool can do

An upscaler draws plausible detail; it does not recover the original pixels, because they no longer exist. Small text, number plates and faces a few pixels wide come back as guesses (sharper, but not necessarily right) so do not treat an upscaled frame as evidence of what was written or who was there. And blur is a different fault from pixelation: a shot that was out of focus or smeared by motion never recorded the detail at all, and no upscaler will find it (how to tell the kinds of blurry apart).

Questions people ask

Can you unpixelate a video?

Not literally: the original pixels are gone. An AI upscaler redraws plausible detail where the blocks were. On a deliberately pixelated 176×144 test clip, Crisp's Max lane held 1.93× the fine detail of a plain upscale.

Is there a free way to fix pixelated video on a Mac?

Yes. Crisp's Enhance lane is free, with a small “Made with Crisp” mark on video exports. Pro removes the mark and adds the Max lane for $129 once, or 4 × $32.25.

Why does my video only look pixelated when things move?

That is compression rather than resolution. The encoder had too few bits for the parts of the frame that changed, so it spent them in blocks. Start from the least-compressed copy you have before upscaling.

Does fixing pixelation make the file bigger?

Usually, yes. An upscaled video has more pixels, and keeping the rebuilt detail takes more data than the blocky original did.

If it is blurry, not pixelated

“Blurry” covers four different problems, and only some can be fixed. Out of focus: not fixable. If the camera focused on the wrong thing, the detail was never recorded; sharpening only raises local contrast and creates halos, so this is a reshoot. Motion blur: not fixable. A fast subject during a long exposure is smeared within each frame, and that smear is the image; frame interpolation can smooth the motion but cannot unsmear the frames. Low resolution: very fixable. Old 480p or 720p clips on a modern display are the ideal case for upscaling: the detail is there, there is just not much of it. Compression damage: partly fixable. Clips sent through messaging apps or downloaded from social platforms lose fine detail and gain blocky artefacts; upscaling helps meaningfully but cannot invent what the encoder discarded, so expect a clear improvement, not a pristine result.

How to tell them apart in ten seconds. Pause on a frame and zoom in. Everything uniformly soft, static background included, points to focus or resolution. Moving subjects smeared while the static background is sharp is motion blur. Blocky squares in flat areas such as sky or shadow is compression. Fine speckle that changes every frame is noise, which is a different fix again.

Sharpening is not upscaling. The sharpen slider raises contrast at existing edges, which makes the detail that is there more visible and makes noise and compression blocks more visible too; push it and you get white halos the eye reads as artificial. Upscaling reconstructs detail that is not in the pixels, using a model trained on what real footage looks like, which is why it can improve a genuinely soft clip where sharpening only makes it crunchier. The limits of the focus case are in why AI upscaling cannot fix focus.
