How to restore old film footage on a Mac

Scanned reels and old tape captures arrive with two problems that have nothing to do with resolution: dirt that flashes for a single frame, and a brightness flicker that makes the whole picture breathe. Here's how to fix both on your Mac, 100% offline — and, just as important, what a program like this honestly can't fix.

Updated August 2026 · step-by-step

What's actually wrong with old footage

It helps to separate the faults, because they need completely different treatment:

  • Single-frame dust and blotches. A speck of dirt on the gate or the scanner glass shows up in one frame and is gone the next. This is the most common defect on a film transfer, and it's the one that can be fixed safely — the frames on either side show what belongs there.
  • Brightness flicker. Frame-to-frame exposure wobble from the original camera, the lamp, or the scanner. The picture pulses even though nothing in the shot changed.
  • Vertical scratches. A tramline running down the film for hundreds of frames. This one is different in kind — see below.
  • Softness and low resolution. A separate job, and the one an upscaler is for.

Two things in that clip are worth pointing out, because they are the honest boundary of what happened. The grain and the single-frame dirt are gone, and the edges of the helmet and collar are cleaner. What you are not seeing is invented detail: the fast lane removes what should not be there rather than imagining what should. Faces stay the faces that were photographed.

The clip is also black and white, which is its own trap. General-purpose upscalers are trained on colour photographs, and handed a neutral grey frame they tend to hand back a faintly warm one. We measured ours doing exactly that — about six levels of blue pulled out of every frame — and now detect a monochrome source and hold the output to it. Your black-and-white footage comes back black and white, not quietly sepia.

The difference is real: the helmet rim, the eyes and the collar come back as defined edges rather than cleaned-up mush. So does the cost, which is why both lanes exist rather than one.

And for archival work there is a caveat worth stating plainly. Max is generative. Where Enhance removes what should not be there, Max reconstructs what a plausible sharper frame would look like — and "plausible" is not the same as "photographed". On a family video that is exactly what you want. On historical footage, on evidence, or on anything a viewer will read as a record of what was actually in front of the lens, that distinction matters, and the honest choice is often the fast lane. We would rather tell you that than sell you the more impressive-looking button.

Every restoration tool sounds convincing in a sentence, so here are the actual test results, including the case that's supposed to fail:

The two "survives" rows are the point. Anyone can delete detail; the difficulty is removing dirt while leaving the picture intact, so we test the opposite case every time.

What this won't do: persistent vertical scratches

Crisp does not remove long vertical scratches, and we'd rather say so than sell you a disappointment.

The reason is worth understanding, because it applies to every automatic tool. Removing a scratch is not the hard part — recognising one is. A tramline that runs down hundreds of frames is, to a filter, indistinguishable from a thin bright vertical thing that genuinely belongs in the shot: a railing, a mast, a window frame, a strip light. We tested this directly, and detection is the wall, not reconstruction. A tool that removes scratches unattended will sooner or later erase something real, in a way you won't notice until you look closely at a shot you cared about.

This is why scratch removal in professional workflows is a supervised, per-scratch job rather than a checkbox. If your footage has them, that's the honest route.

Restoring old film, in detail

Can Crisp remove dust and specks from old film?

Yes — for dirt that sits in a single frame, which is the common case on a transfer. Single-frame specks were cleared in our tests, while a deliberate three-frame mark was left alone. That second result is what stops the filter eating real detail.

Can it remove vertical scratches?

No, and it doesn't claim to. See the section above — the obstacle is telling a scratch apart from a thin object that belongs in the shot.

Will fixing flicker flatten a deliberate fade?

No. The fix targets frame-to-frame exposure jitter and leaves intentional changes alone; a deliberate fade keeps its range in our tests.

Does restoring shorten the clip?

No. Frames in equals frames out and the audio stays in sync — including on clips only one or two frames long.

Does it work with Max quality?

Yes. Both repairs apply on every upscale preset, Max included.

Is my footage uploaded anywhere?

No — it's all done on your Mac, so there's no upload, no queue and no size cap.
