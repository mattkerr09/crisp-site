Denoise vs a plain blur

A simple blur hides noise by smearing everything, softening the whole frame. Crisp uses AI restoration. It removes grain while rebuilding edges and texture, so the result looks clean and sharp rather than mushy. Grain, blocking and banding are different problems

"Noisy" covers three faults that need different fixes. Sensor grain is the fine speckle you get from shooting in low light. That's what denoising is for. Blocking is the mushy squares that appear in heavily compressed footage, which is a bitrate problem, not a noise one. Banding is visible steps in a gradient like a sky, and denoise usually makes it worse.

Look at a still frame before you reach for a slider. Fine speckle that shifts every frame is grain. Chunky squares that hold still for a moment are compression. Smooth stripes across a sky are banding.

What the Denoise value does at each end

The Denoise value runs from 0 to 1 and starts at 0.40. The app sets it for you: 0.6 when you ask it to remove the noise, and the preset's own value otherwise. There is no slider for it in the editor. That starting point is not arbitrary, and knowing why makes the rest of this page easier to act on: 0.5 is a threshold, not just a bigger number.

  • At or below 0.5, cleaning is done by the restoration model alone. It removes grain by rebuilding detail, which is why it does not smear edges the way a blur does.
  • Above 0.5, Crisp adds a conventional temporal-and-spatial denoise pass in front of the model, and its strength scales with the Denoise value. This is the setting that genuinely removes heavy noise, and it is also where waxy skin and plastic hair come from, because a conventional denoiser cannot tell fine detail from fine noise.

So the advice below has a number attached to it. Work up from the default and you are staying model-only until you cross 0.5; the first move past that point is the biggest single change you can make to the result, in both directions.

Low-light behaves differently on purpose. It does not simply brighten and leave the denoise where you left it: on genuinely dark footage it raises the denoise floor to 0.6, above the threshold, because lifting shadows reveals noise that was hiding in them. On moderately dim footage it uses a gentler 0.3 floor and stays model-only. That is why Low-light on a very dark clip can look softer than you expected: it is not the brightening, it is the floor.

Don't over-denoise

Heavy denoising is the most common way to make footage look worse. Push it too far and skin turns waxy, hair becomes a solid mass, and fine texture in fabric or foliage smears into paint. It reads as cheap immediately.

Work up from a light setting rather than down from a strong one, and judge on a still with faces in it, faces are where over-processing shows first.

Choosing and judging a denoiser

Three different things people call “noise”. They have different causes and do not all respond to the same treatment, which is why one tool looks brilliant on someone else’s clip and useless on yours. Luminance grain is the fine salt-and-pepper speckle from a sensor pushed to a high ISO in low light, the kind denoisers handle best. Chroma noise is coloured blotches, usually red and green, in the shadows; it is often easier to remove than grain because colour detail is naturally coarser than brightness detail, so you lose less by smoothing it. Compression mush is blocking and mosquito artefacts baked in by a low bitrate from a messaging app or an old upload. Picture noise and audio noise are separate jobs; for hiss and hum in the sound, see removing background noise.

What denoising costs you. Noise and fine texture live in the same high-frequency part of the image, so any filter strong enough to remove one takes some of the other. The honest question is never “does it remove noise” but “how much texture does it take to get there”. The failure is easy to spot once you know it: faces go waxy, pores and stubble vanish, hair turns into a solid shape, and brick or fabric flattens into a smear. On a small preview that reads as clean; at full size it reads as plastic. Push the strength up until you see that happen, then come back down: the setting just below the plastic point is usually right. Temporal beats spatial, when you have the frames. A spatial denoiser looks at one frame and guesses which speckles are noise. A temporal one compares several neighbouring frames, and since real detail stays put while noise moves randomly, it separates them far more reliably, which is why a clip often cleans up better as video than a single exported still does as a photo. Fast motion is the hard case, because there is less agreement to exploit.

How to judge a denoiser on your own footage. Vendor comparison sliders are chosen to flatter, so test on your worst clip and look at four things. Skin at 100% zoom: pores and fine lines should survive, and an airbrushed face means the setting is too strong. Repeating fine texture such as brick, grass and knitwear, which dissolves first. Shadows, where most noise lives and where most banding appears once it is gone. And playback, not stills: some denoisers leave a faint crawling shimmer that a paused frame never shows.
