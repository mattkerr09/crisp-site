Why the trades are described separately

The controls are identical whichever page you land on. What differs is the footage: a wedding videographer and a game streamer have almost nothing in common in what arrives on the timeline, and the settings that rescue one will ruin the other.

Compressed game capture, for instance, is damaged in a very specific way (blocking in flat areas, smeared motion) and denoising it hard removes exactly the detail worth keeping. Tape transfers have the opposite problem: real grain plus interlacing, where the order of operations decides everything. A church service shot on one locked-off camera in poor light needs almost none of the editing features and all of the low-light ones.

Each entry below names the two or three settings that matter for that kind of footage, and says which parts of the app are not worth your time. If your work spans several of them, read the one whose footage is hardest: that is the one that will decide whether Crisp is useful to you.

What every one of them has in common

Three things come up in every trade below, and they are the reasons people pick this app over a bigger one.

The footage is often the only copy. Wedding and event work cannot be reshot; family tape has no negative; a church service happened once. That changes what counts as an acceptable tool. Crisp never modifies your original file (every operation writes a new one) and it never uploads anything, so a failed job costs you time and nothing else.

The volume is lumpy. Most of these trades do nothing for weeks and then have forty clips to get through in an evening. That is why the queue and batch processing matter more than any individual filter, and why a per-minute or per-export pricing model is punishing for this kind of work.

Nobody wants to become a colourist. The people below have a job that is not video editing, and the video is in the way of it. The measure of a good result here is not how close it gets to a broadcast master; it is whether it looks right to the person who will watch it, done in an evening rather than a weekend.

Eight trades in detail

The write-ups for eight of the trades above, each with what to fix first, what Crisp will not do for that work, and where the footage goes.

Churches and places of worship

A new recording every week whether anyone is ready or not, a volunteer on the camera who may be different from last week, and an archive that goes back further than anybody planned for. That rewards a simple, repeatable workflow far more than a powerful one. The weekly job is the same four steps every time: trim the dead air at the start and end, level the audio so the sermon and the music are both audible, compress the file so it uploads before Tuesday, and cut two or three short clips for social. Crisp’s request box is a router over the operations it actually has: it reads the sentence, proposes a plan and waits for someone to confirm before anything runs, and when asked for something it cannot do it says so rather than running the nearest thing it knows. So the volunteer who was not here last week does not need to remember where a setting lives. It needs macOS 14 (Sonoma) or later, which is worth checking first when the booth Mac is the one nobody has budget to replace; on macOS 14 upscaling takes about 1.5 times as long.

Audio is the whole game. Congregations forgive a soft picture and will not forgive audio they cannot follow: a speaker who moves off the microphone, a music segment far louder than the spoken word, room noise from the back. Levelling the quiet parts up rather than raising everything is what makes a recording listenable on a phone speaker, which is how most of the congregation will watch it; do it before compressing, not after. Normalising sets one overall loudness target and leaves the gap between the sermon and the band where it was, while levelling narrows the gap itself. Take the feed from the sound desk if there is one: a camera microphone at the back of a room with a PA in it is fighting a losing battle, and no processing fixes that.

Low light and long lenses. Sanctuaries are darker than they look, and volunteers often shoot from the back on a long lens, the worst combination for noise and shake. Denoise before any brightening, or you amplify the noise along with the picture; the Low-light cleanup preset does it in that order, so it is one choice rather than a sequence to remember. Stabilization helps genuine handheld movement but will not fix the wobble from a long lens on a cheap tripod head, which is often rolling shutter rather than shake; Crisp measures whether stabilising would actually steady the clip and declines when the answer is no.

The archive nobody has time for. Most churches have a shelf of tapes and DVDs of anniversaries, weddings and funerals going back decades, and it responds well, because the faults are the recoverable kind: low resolution, analogue noise, interlacing. An upscaler run first sharpens the comb artefacts into something much harder to remove, so Crisp reads the combing off the frames (the file’s own flag stops being true once a tape rip has been cut and re-encoded) and straightens it before it upscales. It picks between a straight deinterlace and an inverse telecine, since the wrong one throws away real frames, and proves the choice on a short sample first. Batch is the practical route for a shelf rather than a clip: queue everything and it runs unattended, overnight, with no per-minute billing.

The short clips. Auto-Montage is the part that gives a volunteer the most time back. Choose a style: Highlights pulls the strongest moments, Smart condense keeps the recording in order and trims the dead air out of it, and Music cuts to a track you supply. The target length runs from five seconds to five minutes, so an hour and a half of service comes back as a ninety-second recap in one pass, and there is a real timeline underneath when the automatic cut is not the one you wanted. Privacy is not abstract here: services include children, funerals and baptisms, and Crisp runs entirely on the machine with nothing transmitted, which makes the answer short.

Where Crisp is the wrong tool. It does not stream, so live broadcast needs whatever you already use. It burns in captions you type but does not transcribe speech, which matters because accessibility is a real obligation for many congregations; check that before planning a workflow around it. It has no multicam sync, and it will not fix audio recorded on a camera microphone at the back of a hall.

Independent filmmakers

Crisp is a repair and finishing tool that sits beside Resolve, Premiere or Final Cut rather than replacing them. On your own Mac it denoises and stabilises shots you cannot reshoot, deinterlaces and upscales archive material, cuts trailers and vertical versions, and writes a ProRes 422 master for the edit. It works on faults of degradation, not faults of capture: sensor noise and camera shake yes, missed focus and rolling shutter skew no. It upscales by inventing plausible detail rather than recovering what was shot, so run a ten-second test on your worst shot before you build a schedule around it. It needs macOS 14 (Sonoma) or later (upscaling takes about 1.5 times as long on 14 as on 15 or later) and it is $129 once · or 4 × $32.25, rather than a subscription.

Gamers and streamers

Game capture looks fine while you play and blocky once it has been through a capture encoder and then a platform encoder. That blocking is a bitrate problem, not a resolution one, and it is baked in at capture: an upscaler can make it less obvious but cannot recover what the encoder threw away, so the best fix is a higher capture bitrate next time. Older 720p or 1080p captures going to a 4K timeline do benefit, especially UI text and HUD elements. YouTube gives 4K uploads more bitrate, so upscaling a 1080p capture to 4K before upload often looks visibly cleaner for the viewer. Avoid heavy denoising on dark gameplay footage.

Video podcasters

The honest hierarchy for a video podcast is audio, then lighting, then everything else. If the microphone is two feet away and the room is echoey, no software in this category will save the episode, and if the audio is good, viewers forgive a picture that is merely fine. The format is unusual (very long takes, almost no movement, a face held in frame for an hour), which makes some problems worse and others irrelevant. Consistency, not sharpness: nobody notices a slightly soft picture across an hour, and everybody notices when one camera is warmer than the other and the cut jumps. Noise in a dim room: long static shots make sensor noise very visible, because your eye has an hour to settle on a flat background wall and watch it boil. Compression on flat backgrounds: a plain wall behind a talking head is exactly what encoders handle badly at low bitrates, giving banding and blotching where there is no detail to hide it. Motion blur is a non-issue, since almost nothing moves, so the one thing upscalers genuinely cannot fix rarely applies.

Matching two cameras. Correct each camera separately rather than grading the whole timeline. Two bodies pointed at the same room will disagree: different sensors, different auto white balance, often one facing a window and the other a lamp. Nudge the warmer one cooler until skin tones roughly agree; matching each other matters more than either being objectively correct, because the cut is where a viewer notices. Frame rate has to match at the shoot, not in post: a 60fps camera cut against a 30fps one has visibly different motion and there is no fix afterwards.

The archive case. A back catalogue shot at 720p is one of the better uses of upscaling: static framing and a consistent subject are the easy end of the problem, and a 2× jump to 1440p or 1080p is well within what a model reconstructs convincingly. Check one episode carefully before batching a hundred. Faces are where a weak upscaler shows itself, with skin smoothed to plastic or a fine crawling shimmer in the background wall that only appears in motion, so watch a minute at full size, playing, before committing the whole archive.

Guests, consent, and where the file goes. Podcast footage is people talking candidly, including material that gets cut precisely because it was said off the cuff. A browser-based editor means all of it, including the parts that never air, sits on a third-party server. Crisp processes on your Mac, so nothing is uploaded: a non-issue for most shows, and for any show that discusses anything sensitive, the whole ballgame. Where Crisp is the wrong tool: if you cut two or three angles against a synced audio track every week, use a proper NLE (Final Cut, Premiere or Resolve) and keep Crisp for the cleanup pass; its timeline is a single track and it would be a bad multicam editor. Descript is also genuinely better at transcript-driven editing, which for interview shows is a real advantage, though it is a subscription and not offline.

Product and e-commerce video

Product video has a different success criterion from almost everything else. Nobody praises your shot selection; the video works if the item is clearly visible, the colour is accurate, the file loads fast, and the two hundred other listings look like they came from the same shop. Consistency and throughput, rather than craft. The throughput problem. One product video is easy; a catalogue is where people lose their evenings, applying the same crop, grade and compression over and over. Batch processing is the whole answer, and it is worth structuring the shoot around it: every item the same way, at the same distance, under the same light, so one recipe applies to all of them. Footage that varies shot to shot forces you back into per-item decisions, which is the cost you were trying to avoid. Batch is part of the paid unlock, and the practical constraint is your own shoot.

Colour accuracy matters more here than anywhere. In most genres colour is taste; in e-commerce it is a returns problem, because a jumper that photographs slightly warmer than it is generates complaints and refunds. The measures are unglamorous: shoot under consistent light rather than mixed daylight and tungsten, include a reference in a test frame, and grade to accurate rather than pleasing. If your camera shoots HDR by default, footage renders differently across devices unless it is converted properly, which is a silent way to end up with inaccurate colour without ever making a colour decision.

One master, many shapes. The same video usually needs to exist as 16:9 for the listing page, 9:16 for stories and short-form, and often 1:1 for a feed. Shoot wide enough to survive the crop, keep the full-frame master, and generate the shapes from it. For a centred product on a plain background, cropping keeps full resolution; for anything where composition matters, filling the frame with a blurred background loses nothing and reads as deliberate. File size is a conversion metric: product video is often watched on a phone on a weak connection by someone deciding whether to buy, and a file that takes four seconds to start is one many people never watch. Compression is part of the job, and footage on a plain background compresses extremely well because most of the frame is not changing.

Where Crisp is the wrong tool. It does not remove backgrounds or generate cutouts (if isolating the product is core to your workflow, that lives elsewhere and Crisp handles the finishing afterwards), it does not do motion graphics, animated price overlays or template-driven branding, and it will not rescue a badly lit shot: no restoration recovers detail from a blown highlight on a glossy surface, the most common product photography failure. Upscaling an old product video helps meaningfully if it is soft because it is low resolution, and not at all if it missed focus, where re-shooting is cheaper than the render time. What it does is make the footage consistent, correctly sized, correctly coloured and small enough to load, in batches, without uploading a product line that has not launched yet to somebody else’s server.

Real estate listings

A listing video does one job: making a property look like somewhere worth visiting. Most agent footage is shot on a phone, walking, in mixed indoor light, which produces three fixable problems. Stabilisation matters more than resolution. Handheld walking footage is the signature of an amateur listing, and a stabilised 1080p walkthrough looks more professional than shaky 4K, every time. Shoot in 4K if your phone allows, because the extra pixels give the stabiliser room to crop in without softening the delivered image, then deliver 1080p. Fix the verticals. Door frames, window edges and worktops are dead straight in reality, so a camera held one degree off reads instantly as sloppy even to someone who could not say why; straighten before anything else, the cheapest credibility in property video.

Windows are the exposure problem. Point a phone at a room with a bright window and it either exposes for the window and turns the room into a cave, or exposes for the room and blows the window to white. Expose for the room, because buyers want to see the room, then lift the shadows slightly in the edit rather than trying to recover a window clipped to pure white, because that detail was never recorded. Keep it short and consistent: portals and social feeds punish long walkthroughs, and sixty to ninety seconds covering the main rooms outperforms a four-minute tour of every cupboard. If you list regularly, use the same order and the same look on every property; a recognisable style across listings does more for an agent’s brand than any single video.

A repeatable ten-minute edit: import the walkthrough clips and drop the unusable takes; stabilise anything shot walking; straighten tilted shots; trim each room to its best five to ten seconds; apply one consistent look across all clips; lift shadows slightly on interiors; export 1080p, and a vertical crop if you post to social. Vertical is not optional any more. Most property discovery happens on a phone and portals increasingly surface vertical clips. Shooting 4K horizontal gives enough pixels to crop a clean 1080×1920 version without it looking soft, so one shoot delivers both. Crop deliberately rather than centring: a centre crop of a room shot from the doorway usually lands on a blank wall when you wanted the window or the kitchen island.

Music and voiceover. Silent walkthroughs feel unfinished, and phone audio in an empty room is mostly echo and footsteps; muting the clip and laying simple music underneath is nearly always better than the original sound. If you narrate, record the voiceover separately in a quiet room rather than talking while you walk. Client footage should not be uploaded. Property interiors are people’s homes, often still occupied, with their belongings in shot; running those files through a browser upscaler means uploading the inside of someone’s house to a third party. Crisp runs on your Mac with no upload and no account, the appropriate default for footage you were trusted with.

Teachers and educators

Teaching video has an unusual constraint: most tooling assumes you are making something polished for strangers, when what you need is something clear, quick to produce and safe to share within an institution. Crisp cleans up teaching video you have already recorded, entirely on your own Mac: it trims an hour down to the twelve minutes that matter, compresses a lecture so it will upload, brings a quiet question up until it is audible, and upscales soft footage. Four problems come up constantly. The file is too big to send: a one-hour lecture can be several gigabytes and mail systems and LMS uploads reject it, but screen-heavy content compresses far better than people expect, because a static slide costs the encoder almost nothing. The text in the screen recording is soft: usually not a resolution problem. Video stores colour at a quarter the detail of brightness (chroma subsampling), so coloured text on a contrasting background is the worst case, and recording at a higher resolution helps more than raising the bitrate. An hour of which twelve minutes matter: trimming first is the biggest time saver, and Crisp’s trim re-encodes rather than stream-copying, so the cut lands on the frame you asked for instead of the nearest keyframe a second or two away. The audio is quiet in places: a lapel microphone that drifted, a question from the back of the room.

Getting a lecture under the upload limit. Compression is a quality ladder, not a size solver. There are three levels (light, medium and hard), each targeting a fraction of the source file’s own bitrate: roughly 70, 50 and 30 per cent of it. Frame size and frame rate are left alone unless you name a smaller size (“make it 720p”), and the file is re-encoded as HEVC through Apple’s VideoToolbox, so the hard setting is about a third the size, not a tenth. Ask it to get the file under 25 MB for an LMS and it declines rather than guessing: it compresses by quality rather than aiming at a file size, so it cannot promise that number, and there is no two-pass bitrate solver behind it. Run the hard setting, read the finished size in the Jobs panel, and trim further if it is still too big. For a semester of recordings, batch runs a folder unattended and skips what it has already done; batch is a paid capability.

The audio is what students complain about. Picture quality is forgiven; audio that cannot be followed is not. Normalise sets one overall loudness target and leaves the gap between your voice at the whiteboard and at the lectern where it was; levelling narrows that gap itself. On a synthetic quiet-then-loud clip, the levelling pass lifted the quiet passage by 16 dB and brought the loud-to-quiet gap from 22 dB down to 19.5 dB without clipping. Both halves get louder (it is a compressor with makeup gain and a limiter, not a selective boost) but the quiet half gains more, which decides whether a question from the back row survives a laptop speaker. The phrasing routes the request: “make the quiet parts louder”, “even out the loud and quiet” and “level the dialogue” reach the levelling pass, while “level the audio” and “even out the volume” reach the overall-loudness one. Steady room noise such as a projector fan or air handling is a separate pass.

Student footage and where it goes. Footage with identifiable students frequently cannot be uploaded to a third-party service, and the answer to “where is this processed” needs to be simple enough to put in an email to a head of department. Crisp processes everything on the machine: no upload, no account, nothing transmitted. Two limits to settle before you plan around them. Crisp cannot blur or pixelate a face; asked, it answers that it has no blur or pixelate tool and refuses rather than running something adjacent, so if a face has to come out, that job belongs in an editor with a mask and a tracker. And it cannot caption a recording: it burns in a caption you type, with no speech recognition of any kind. Accessibility captioning is a legal obligation in most institutions, so plan the transcript through a transcription service or your institution’s system and bring the text across.

A workflow that fits a prep period: trim to what matters, compress until it uploads, bring the quiet parts up, export. All four can be asked for in plain English, which matters when the tool is used once a fortnight; the request box lists the steps it proposes on a card and waits for you to press Run it before anything renders. When an hour needs to become a five-minute recap rather than a trim, Auto-Montage’s Smart condense keeps the recording in order and cuts the dead air out of it. Where Crisp is the wrong tool: it does not record your screen, it has no multicam sync (a room camera and a document camera cut together belong in an NLE), and it is not a course authoring tool, so quizzes, chaptering and LMS packaging live elsewhere.

Wedding videographers

Crisp is an offline Mac app for the repair and finishing passes around a wedding edit, not for the edit itself. Wedding work has a shape no other genre shares: one take of everything, light that goes from a bright ceremony to a dark reception in a day, half the footage from a camera you were not operating, and a client emotionally invested in every frame. The reception is where the footage falls apart. By evening you shoot at whatever ISO keeps the shutter usable, and that means the coloured speckle in the shadows that compression turns into blotches. Denoising before you grade rather than after makes a visible difference, because a grade applied to noisy footage amplifies the noise with everything else. In Crisp the denoise control decides which model runs, routing to the cleaning net rather than the detail net as you turn it up, and past halfway it also runs a noise-reduction pass before the upscale; the Low-light cleanup preset is that choice already made, with a shadow lift as well. Dancing shots and walking shots need stabilization, which helps genuinely with shake but not with rolling shutter skew, the usual cause of wobble on fast pans. Crisp measures whether stabilising would actually steady the clip and declines when the answer is no, so a tripod ceremony shot or a smooth pan comes back untouched instead of worse.

Two cameras that do not match. A second shooter, a locked-off camera at the back and a phone clip someone sent afterwards: three colour renderings, three noise characteristics, often three frame rates. Normalise before you cut, not after, getting everything to the same frame rate and colour range first, then grade; otherwise the grade looks right on the A camera and wrong every time you cut away. Crisp does that on the way into a montage or a timeline without being asked: every segment is scaled onto one common canvas, resampled to one constant frame rate and converted into a single known colour space rather than merely re-tagged (the usual cause of an old SD clip going pale in the middle of a film), with any HDR or 10-bit clip tonemapped to SDR so it does not sit washed out beside the others. Phone footage is the frequent offender because it is often variable frame rate and often HDR; a variable-rate source is detected from its own timing and forced to a constant rate, which keeps audio from drifting out of sync across a cut. Matching the creative look of two different sensors is still a grading decision, and it is much easier once the technical differences are gone.

The teaser, and the edit behind it. The short cut is usually the piece the couple shares first, and the one that eats an evening you would rather spend on the film. Auto-Montage takes a long recording and a target length from five seconds to five minutes, in three styles: Highlights picks the strongest moments, Condense keeps the recording in order and trims the dead air out, and Music cuts to a track you supply. Layouts run from a straight sequential reel to split-screen and a four-up grid. Under the automatic cut is a real timeline: drag clips in, drag edges to trim, reorder, repeat a clip, drop a photo in as a title card with a Ken Burns move or held still, and burn in captions from text you type. Reframing to 9:16, 1:1 or 4:5 fits the whole frame inside the new shape and fills the bars with a blurred copy of the picture rather than black, so a horizontal first dance becomes a vertical cutdown without cropping the couple out of it.

The delivery problem nobody warns you about. You grade a film carefully and the couple watches it on a platform that re-encodes everything you sent: gradients band, the dress goes blotchy in the highlights, and the grain you added reads as noise. Clean the footage before delivery so the encoder spends its budget on faces rather than noise, and deliver at a higher resolution than strictly needed, because platforms allocate bitrate by resolution: a 1080p film uploaded as 4K frequently survives better than the same film uploaded at 1080p. When the file has to get smaller rather than better, the compress lane targets a fraction of the source’s own bitrate and leaves dimensions and frame rate alone, writing MP4 and MOV only.

The couple’s parents’ tapes. Clients hand you a shoebox more often than you would expect: a VHS of the bride’s parents’ wedding, a camcorder tape of a childhood birthday. That material responds well, because its faults are the recoverable kind. Almost all of it is interlaced, and sharpening combing rather than removing it makes the artefact far harder to get rid of afterwards, so an upscale straightens the combing first, working out what to straighten from the frames rather than the file’s flag. It also tells video that was shot interlaced apart from film that went through a telecine, because the wrong fix throws away real frames: it proves the inverse telecine on a sample before committing and falls back to a plain deinterlace otherwise. It lifts single-frame dust and specks off a film transfer, but it will not take out the long vertical scratches, and says so rather than smearing the picture trying: a scratch sits in the same place frame after frame, which makes it unfixable unattended.

Client media, and where Crisp is the wrong tool. Wedding footage is intimate, includes children and elderly relatives, and couples increasingly ask where their files are processed. Crisp runs everything on your Mac with no upload, no account and no cloud step, and an overnight batch costs electricity rather than per-minute cloud billing, which matters when a wedding is three hundred gigabytes of source. It is not an NLE: a full film with multicam sync, audio sweetening and a client review round belongs in Resolve or Premiere, its timeline is a single track of ordered clips, and it cannot align a separate audio recorder’s file to the picture. It has no speech recognition, so it cannot subtitle the vows or the speeches (the transcript has to come from a transcription tool first), and no blur or pixelate tool, so a guest who does not want to be on camera has to be handled with a cut rather than a mask. Nor can it rescue a shot that missed focus, recover a blown highlight on a white dress, or fix a ceremony where the audio was never recorded. Nothing can, and knowing that before you promise a fix is worth more than any feature.
