Crisp for churches and places of worship

Crisp · built in Grand Rapids · published 2026-08-11 · last updated 2026-09-15
Quick answer

Crisp cleans up the recordings a church media team already has, on the Mac in the booth. It levels a sermon so quiet speech is audible against the worship band, denoises dark sanctuary footage, trims and compresses a livestream recording into something that will upload, cuts short clips for social, and upscales the tape archive. Nothing is uploaded and there is no account.

Church video has a shape almost no commercial workflow shares. There is a new recording every week whether anyone is ready or not, the person operating the camera is a volunteer who may be different from last week, and the archive goes back further than anybody planned for.

That combination rewards workflows that are simple and repeatable far more than ones that are powerful.

The weekly job

Most of the work is the same four steps every time: trim the dead air at the start and end, level the audio so the sermon and the music are both audible, compress the file so it will upload before Tuesday, and cut two or three short clips for social.

Being able to ask for those in plain English matters more here than in most settings, because the person doing it this week may not have done it last week. A workflow that needs remembering where a setting lives is a workflow that breaks the moment the usual volunteer is away. Crisp's request box is a router over the operations it actually has: it reads the sentence, proposes a plan, and waits for someone to confirm before anything runs. Ask it for something Crisp cannot do and it says so rather than running the nearest thing it knows. It installs on macOS 12 and later, which matters when the booth Mac is the one nobody has budget to replace.

Audio is the whole game

Congregations forgive a soft picture and will not forgive audio they cannot follow. The common faults are a speaker who moves off the microphone, a music segment far louder than the spoken word, and room noise from the back.

Levelling the quiet parts up rather than raising everything is what makes a recording listenable on a phone speaker, which is how most of the congregation will actually watch it. Do that before compressing, not after.

Levelling and normalising are different controls, and the difference matters for a service. Normalising sets one overall loudness target and leaves the gap between the sermon and the band where it was; levelling narrows the gap itself, so the spoken word comes up without the music getting louder still.

Take the feed from the sound desk if there is one. A camera microphone at the back of a room with a PA in it is fighting a losing battle, and no processing fixes that.

Low light and long lenses

Sanctuaries are usually darker than they look, and volunteers often shoot from the back on a long lens, which is the worst combination for both noise and shake. Denoise before any brightening, or you amplify the noise along with the picture — the Low-light cleanup preset does it in that order, a stronger denoise ahead of the upscale, so it is one choice rather than a sequence to remember. Stabilization helps genuine handheld movement; it will not fix the wobble from a long lens on a cheap tripod head, which is often rolling shutter rather than shake. Crisp measures whether stabilising would actually steady the clip in front of it and declines when the answer is no.

The archive nobody has time for

Most churches have a shelf of tapes and DVDs of anniversaries, weddings and funerals going back decades. That material is genuinely worth restoring, and it responds well, because the faults are the recoverable kind: low resolution, analogue noise, interlacing.

Almost all of it will be interlaced, and an upscaler run first sharpens the comb artefacts into something much harder to remove. Crisp reads that off the frames rather than the file's own flag, which stops being true the moment a tape rip has been cut and re-encoded, and straightens the combing itself before it upscales. It picks between a straight deinterlace and an inverse telecine — the wrong one throws away real frames — and proves the choice on a short sample first. Nobody in the booth has to know which a given tape needs.

Batch is the practical route for a shelf rather than a clip: queue everything and it runs unattended, videos and photos both. Because it all runs on the machine there is no per-minute billing on a job that might take all night.

The short clips, and the edit behind them

Auto-Montage is the part that gives a volunteer the most time back. Point it at the service recording and choose a style: Highlights pulls the strongest moments, Smart condense keeps the recording in order and trims the dead air out of it, and Music cuts to a track you supply. The target-length slider runs from five seconds to five minutes, so an hour-and-a-half of service comes back as a ninety-second recap in a single pass.

When the automatic cut is not the one you wanted, there is a real timeline underneath it. Drag clips in, drag their edges to trim, drag the middle to reorder, drop a photo in as a title card. Captions burn in from text you type. Those three — a timeline, typed captions and an auto-montage — are the ones no other Mac upscaler we have read ships at all, and they are why the weekly job stays in one app rather than three.

Privacy is not an abstract concern here

Services include children, funerals, baptisms and people who did not choose to be filmed. Uploading that to a third-party service to process it is a conversation most churches would rather not have. Crisp runs entirely on the machine, with nothing transmitted, which makes the answer short.

Where Crisp is the wrong tool

It does not stream, so live broadcast needs whatever you already use. It does not generate captions from speech, which matters because accessibility is a real obligation for many congregations, and that needs a transcription service. It has no multicam sync, so a genuinely multi-camera edit belongs in an NLE. And it will not fix audio recorded on a camera microphone at the back of a hall.

What it does is the weekly cleanup, the short clips, and the archive, without any of it leaving the building.

Service recordings, in detail

Can Crisp add captions to our service recordings?

It burns in captions you have typed, but it does not transcribe speech. If captions are an accessibility requirement, you need a transcription service to produce the text first. That is worth checking before planning a workflow around it.

How do we make the sermon audible on a phone?

Level the quiet parts up rather than raising the overall volume, and do it before compressing. Most of a congregation watches on a phone speaker, and levelling makes far more difference there than any picture improvement.

How do we cut a short clip out of a ninety-minute service?

Use Auto-Montage. Highlights picks the strongest moments, Smart condense keeps the service in order and trims the dead air out of it, and Music cuts to a track you supply. The target length goes from five seconds to five minutes, and anything it chose can be adjusted afterwards on the timeline.

Is it practical to restore an archive of old service tapes?

Yes, and it is one of the better uses for it. The faults on old tape are the recoverable kind. Work through it in batches, deinterlace before upscaling, and expect it to run overnight rather than in minutes.

Does any footage leave our machine?

No. There is no upload, no account and no cloud step, which for recordings involving children and funerals is usually the deciding factor rather than a bonus.
