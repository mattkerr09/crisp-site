Is Topaz Video AI free?

By Matt Kerr, who makes Crisp in Grand Rapids · published 2026-09-28 · last updated 2026-11-10
Quick answer

No. Topaz Video (the app formerly called Topaz Video AI) is sold as a subscription: $299 a year or $59 a month for the desktop app, and Topaz's own pricing page lists no free plan (topazlabs.com/pricing, read 2 November 2026). Since 23 September 2026 Topaz Labs has belonged to Adobe, which says the Topaz apps continue as standalone products. The free option on a Mac: Crisp’s free version upscales offline, with a small mark on video exports, and took a 40-second 1080p phone clip to 4K in 3 min 26 s on my M1 Ultra (Crisp 1.0.1777, 2 November 2026).

How it got here

Topaz used to sell a perpetual licence. It stopped on 3 October 2025, and the app was renamed from Topaz Video AI to Topaz Video along the way, which is why so many people still search for the old name (what changed, and when). Then, on 23 September 2026, Adobe completed its purchase of Topaz Labs. Adobe says the apps carry on as standalone products (its announcement, read 2 November 2026); what that means for pricing later is not something anyone outside the two companies can know yet.

Free and one-time options on a Mac

Crisp is the one this site is about, so weigh that. Its free tier runs the full editor and the standard AI enhancement, and video exports carry a small “crispvideo.app” mark, about 12% of the frame's width, bottom right; photos come out unmarked. Pro removes the mark and adds the generative Max lane and batch processing. Launch price: $14.50 once, through November 30. From December 1: $29. It runs offline: your videos never leave your computer.

Measured on a 3.75-second, 90-frame film clip, Crisp's free Enhance lane upscaled it 4× in 13.0 seconds. The generative Max lane took 30.3 seconds a frame on the same clip (slow, and on video it needs a Mac with 48 GB of memory or more) but on a deliberately degraded 176×144 phone clip it held 1.93× the fine detail of a plain upscale.

Open-source image upscalers such as Real-ESRGAN, and free apps built on it, cost nothing and work well on single images (their own READMEs, read 2 November 2026). Using them on video means splitting it into frames and putting it back together, which is where the time and the disk space go (what that route costs, measured).

What Topaz still does better

Topaz has a deeper catalogue of models and more control over each one, a Windows version, and a long record in professional restoration work (its pricing page names the models and says Mac and PC, read 2 November 2026). If you need those, a subscription can be worth it. If what you want is to make old or low-resolution video look better on your own Mac without a yearly bill, a free tier and a one-time price are a different trade, and the full comparison sets the two side by side.
