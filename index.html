Real output

Measured, not claimed.

Where the clip at the top of this page came from, what it scored, and a second example that flatters us — labelled as such.

The hard case — the clip at the top of this page. Source: Farewell to Treasure Island (1940), the closing days of the Golden Gate International Exposition, from the Prelinger Archives at the Internet Archive under a Creative Commons public-domain dedication. This is a real archival scan at 640×480, not a clean file we degraded on purpose — it is soft and grainy because 1940 film digitised in the 1990s is soft and grainy. The clip at the top is this film run through Max, the generative restore, on Apple Silicon — a restore at 1280×720 rather than an upscale, so both sides of the slider are the same size and only the detail differs. Max works a frame at a time and it is not free: a single frame takes about 52 seconds on an M1 Ultra, and longer runs only amortise so far — five frames cost 51.6 seconds each, thirteen 44.2, and this 132-frame clip 32.9, which is still 72 minutes for four seconds of film. Look at the row of small blocks under the eave: scaled up they are one smear, and Max separates them into individual blocks with light and shadow on each. The free Enhance lane upscales the same clip 4× to 2560×1920 in 36 seconds, which is the trade and why Enhance is the default. Both clips play unedited at the same size, from the same four seconds, and both were compressed for the web with identical settings, so neither side is flattered. On a Retina display you are watching the full 2560-wide file. Measured on the roof detail as mean absolute Laplacian, the Max frame carries 1.50× the fine detail of the source it was given.

SourceCrisp 16×

The easy case, and we will say why. A city at night from the International Space Station, NASA, public domain. Unlike the clip above, this one was degraded on purpose: a sharp original reduced to 426×224 to stand in for low-resolution footage, then upscaled 16× to 6816×3584 and shown here at 1704×896. That is a much kinder test than real archival film — the detail was genuinely there to begin with — and against a plain resize to the same delivered size it scores 3.42× the fine detail, where the archival clip above manages 1.50×. Both are shown because one without the other tells you half the truth: this is what Crisp does when the source cooperates, and the clip above is what it does when the source does not.

Past 4×, and who it is for. The upscaling networks are trained at 2×, 3× and 4×, so anything larger has to be a chain of real passes rather than one bigger number: 8× is a 4× pass followed by a 2× pass, and 16× is 4× twice. Each pass gets the network built for it, which is why 8× is not simply 16× stopped early. On the same 160×120 crop, 4× takes 0.6 seconds, 8× takes 1.0, and 16× takes 1.5.

Side by side, 4×, 8× and 16× of the same crop look nearly identical, so there is no panel of them here. The extra passes buy pixel dimensions, not visibly more detail — 16× is for when you need the size, and past 8× the picture stops changing much.

These are for small sources, and Crisp will tell you so. The big multipliers exist for the footage that needs them: tape captures, old camcorder files, 480p archive scans. A 640×480 clip at 16× is a 10240×7680 file and encodes fine. A 1080p clip at 8× would be 15360×8640, which is past what the Mac video encoder will write at all — so Crisp greys those out against the clip you actually loaded and shows the finished size next to the picker, rather than letting you find out after the render.

Auto-montage

Three minutes in.
Twenty-seven seconds out.

Nobody watches the whole tape. Point Crisp at a long clip and it finds the moments worth keeping, cuts them together and hands you the result — or drops them on the timeline if you would rather finish by hand.

This is the unedited output. Same film as above — Farewell to Treasure Island, 174 seconds of it — asked for a 30-second highlights reel. Crisp picked 11 moments in 9.5 seconds of analysis: the fair from the water, both bridges, the tower, the arch, the crowds on the concourse, the pagoda garden, the Gayway at night. Nothing was chosen by hand and nothing was trimmed afterwards. It is upscaled 2× for this page, because the source is a 640×480 scan.

It reads the footage rather than the clock. Motion, audio, where the attention falls in frame, and whether the shot is in focus all feed the choice, and a golf swing, a game capture and a stadium sport each get a different weighting. Captions and fades are skipped — an early version of this reel opened on four seconds of title card, which is the sort of thing you only find by running it on real film.

FAQ

Clear answers.

Is it really 100% offline?

Yes. Crisp runs entirely on your Mac. No upload, no account, no queue, and it works with the Wi-Fi switched off. The only things that ever touch the network are checking for an update, activating a licence, and downloading the optional Max model pack, never your footage.

How is this different from Topaz?

Topaz moved to a subscription, and its Starlight restore models need either macOS 26 or 36 GB of memory depending on the variant. Crisp is a one-time-owned, fully-offline app with a generative-restore lane on-device, an AUTO mode instead of a 15-model picker, and interpolation that keeps your audio. The bigger difference is shape: Topaz's own docs list trim and crop, so it takes a file and hands a file back. Crisp puts the upscale inside an edit — arrange, trim, split and reorder clips on a timeline, cut them to a track, then export the whole cut at 4K.

Do I need a powerful Mac?

Any Apple-Silicon Mac runs the fast tier (1080p → 4K at interactive speed). The generative Max tier is an optional 4.6 GB download and is far slower than the word “overnight” suggests. On video it is a desktop feature: a clip pushes peak memory past 27 GB, so it wants a 48 GB Mac at the very least and is only comfortable on 64 GB. On a single photo there is no run of frames to hold at once, peak drops to about 11 GB, and a 16 GB Air handles it. Measured on an M1 Ultra, a Max frame costs about 36 seconds per megapixel of output — 33 seconds a frame at 720p, and around 74 at 1080p. That is roughly 3 hours for five seconds of 1080p, 18 hours for thirty seconds, and three days for two minutes. Crisp shows you that estimate before the job starts, not three minutes into it. Treat Max as a seconds-at-a-time tool for the one shot that matters — Enhance is the lane for whole clips.

Will it strip my audio or drift out of sync?

No. Audio is stream-copied and never re-timed, even when you interpolate to a higher frame rate. Color is converted and re-tagged correctly, so nothing comes out washed-out.

What about damaged, dark, or noisy footage?

That's exactly the target. Crisp denoises, lifts shadows, and restores detail before it upscales, so low-light and compressed clips come out clean instead of amplifying the mess.

Does it do photos and editing too?

Yes. Drop a JPEG, PNG, or HEIC and Crisp upscales & restores the still in about a second. And the built-in Montage mode auto-edits a long video into a highlight reel, a tightened condense, or a music montage cut to the beat — all on-device, no timeline required.
